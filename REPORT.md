# Report: Storyboard to Screen

Repository: https://github.com/Jima-J9M1/storyboard-to-screen

Status of this report: written on 26 November 2026 from the evidence in the repository. Section 2 is waiting on the director, because it records what people said and only the director can supply that.

## 1. What I set out to make, and for whom

A 60–90 second film, **The Last Buna** (`films/the-last-buna`), narrated in Amharic with English subtitles. It is a made-up story set in Jimma: a mother pours the third and last cup of the coffee ceremony, the one called baraka, every morning for a son who left for Addis Ababa seven years ago, until the morning he comes home and pours it for her.

- **Audience:** Ethiopians, especially young adults living away from their families, and viewers abroad who are curious about Ethiopian life, watching on a phone.
- **Feeling at the end:** love and longing for a parent. However far you go, do not forget the person who is waiting for you at home.

Both were written into the film's `brief.json` before its first model call, and are in `DESIGN.md`.

I also set out to build something more general than one film. The repository is a system that takes a title, or a story told in the director's own words, and produces a film: agents draft a storyboard, a person directs it, and code renders it. The Last Buna is the third film made with it. The first, about the plant scientist Gebisa Ejeta, is complete and is where most of the pipeline's lessons were learned; the second, about Abebe Bikila, is a rough cut.

The finished film is 68.5 seconds in 9:16 and 16:9, at -14.65 LUFS, with 12 shots: nine are video generated by Wan 2.2 and three are camera moves over a picture (`films/the-last-buna/outputs/film/report.json`).

## 3. The hardest problem, and how I solved it

This problem was met on the first film, Gebisa Ejeta, and its solution is part of the pipeline every film now uses.

**The agent that built the pipeline cannot hear or watch what it makes.**

Every check on the film had to be turned into something measurable in text. That mattered most with the narration:

1. The first recording of ten lines took 122 seconds, where the plan allowed about 70. It looked like slow speech.
2. Measuring the silence in each file ruled out padding. Speaking one line four ways, changing only the style instruction, showed the line took 6.9 seconds with no instruction and 12.6 with one.
3. Having a second model transcribe the audio gave the answer: the narrator was reading its own stage direction aloud. Every line began "Say in a calm, warm, unhurried voice."
4. Writing the style in square brackets fixed it. The same check then caught a second fault that sounded fine on paper: two of the ten lines had been spoken by a different voice, because a fallback had been used silently when the free tier refused calls.

The voice stage now transcribes every file it makes and compares it with its line, and it never substitutes a narrator (`FAILURES.md` F4 and F5; `EXPERIMENTS.md` E2).

The solution has a limit, and the Amharic films found it. In Amharic the transcript and the script agree on only about half the words in some lines, and there is no way to tell from text whether the voice or the transcriber is wrong (E8). For Amharic a person has to listen. The submitted film shows it: with the better models refusing calls, a weaker one transcribed shot 2's Amharic as English-sounding nonsense, and the check reported extra words that are not in the audio.

## 4. Suggested practices, tested

Both cases are from the first film; both changed how the submitted film was made.

**One that beat my instinct: time the narration before making pictures.**
The Screenwriter planned shot lengths at 2.3 words a second, the middle of the range the assignment quotes. The recorded narration ran at 1.82. Seven of ten lines did not fit their shots, and the film needed 94.5 seconds against a 90-second limit. Had pictures and clips been made to the planned lengths, every one would have been wrong. Because the narration came first, the fix was to trim silence and set each shot's length from its recorded line, which brought the film to 89.5 seconds without cutting a word (E3; `films/gebisa-ejeta/outputs/narration/timings.json`). The Last Buna was made the same way: its 12 shot lengths were set from the recorded Amharic lines, which ran at 1.51 words a second, before any clip was generated.

**One that did not: generate every keyframe against the reference sheet.**
With the reference sheet passed to the picture model as a guide image, the two wide landscape shots came back as copies of the sheet, glass flasks included, instead of bare feet on a path and a field of sorghum. Stronger wording did not stop it. With no guide image the same shots showed what was asked, and the style block alone kept the look consistent. Each shot now has a `use_reference` setting, off for wide shots and on for close and medium ones (E4). Applied to every shot, as suggested, the practice produced two unusable pictures out of ten. In The Last Buna the same thing happened in a new form: the reference picture contains a woman, and she appeared, face showing, in close shots that were meant to show only hands. Five shots were remade without the reference.

## 5. Work beyond the floor

**A tool for others, not one film.** Films live in their own folders, every command takes a film's name, and a new film starts from a title or from a story typed into the production board (Next.js). The board shows every film and what each stage made, starts stages, and passes a director's note on one picture or line to a Reviser agent that rewrites that one field. A `Dockerfile` runs the board and pipeline on a server, read-only unless a password is set (`deploy/README.md`).

**A story no website tells.** The Last Buna has no public source. The director's telling is recorded as the source, and the same quote check applies: each of its 21 facts quotes the telling word for word. Building it exposed a fault: the check that stops a protected person being named in a picture treated the title's words, "last" and "buna", as a name and rejected every draft. A told story's title is no longer treated as a name (`subject_name` in `agents/story.py`).

**Generated video on a free GPU, and its limits.** Wan 2.2 runs on a 15 GB Kaggle T4 at about 12 to 13 minutes a clip. For this film, 22 clips took two GPU sessions of 153 and 167 minutes. Four frames of every clip were checked before use. Three clips were wrong (a hand changed skin tone, cups multiplied, a hand removed cups it should have set down), and those shots use a camera move instead. On the first film, a second clip made to extend a shot turned a person to face the camera (F10).

**A seed is not a guarantee.** In the walkthrough's live regeneration, shot s03 was redrawn with a changed prompt, then with the original prompt and the original seed. The second picture was not the original. On this hosted model the same seed and prompt do not return the same picture, so the film's stability comes from the cache, which never redraws a picture whose inputs are unchanged, not from the seed alone.

**A second language.** Amharic is spoken more slowly than the English it was translated from (1.51 words a second here), so timing cannot be carried over from an English script, and the automatic check on narration does not work in Amharic (E8).

## 6. What it cost

`COSTS.md` is generated from the run log.

| | |
|---|---|
| Attempts at outside calls, all three films | 529, of which 227 succeeded |
| Paid | 0.07 USD (11.25 ETB by the log) for 22 Amharic narration calls to Addis AI, across two films. Everything else used a free tier. |
| GPU time for this film's video | 153 and 167 minutes on a Tesla T4 |
| GPU time for this film's three score candidates | 5.0 minutes |

More than half of all attempts failed, almost all because a free tier refused the call. Across the three films, the Script stage needed 103 attempts for 12 successes. The retry, fallback and cache code is what turned that into a finished film.

The Addis AI voice is not in the assignment's free stack. It was used because the free Gemini voice quota was exhausted (F7). The committed audio is reused, so a clean clone rebuilds the film with no key and no call.

## 7. What I would build next

1. **A human sign-off for narration in each language**, recorded in the storyboard, since the automatic check cannot be trusted outside English.
2. **An automatic check of generated clips**: sample frames from every clip and flag a visible face or a changed object count, which today a person must look for.
3. **A quota budget.** The pipeline should count calls against each free tier's daily limit and say before it starts whether a film will fit.
4. **A female Amharic narrator** for this film; the voice used is a man's.
5. **A hosted board.** The container is built and tested locally, but the free host tried no longer runs containers without a subscription.

## What is not done

- The two user tests are not recorded.
- The score in the film (candidate 1) was not chosen by ear; the director's choice and reason are not recorded.
- The Amharic narration has not been confirmed by a person listening to every line.
- The film does not say on screen that the story is made up.
- One picture has a known error: the cup in shot s05 has a handle.
- The clean-clone rebuild was last measured on the first film (E7), not on this one.
